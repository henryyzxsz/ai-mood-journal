# Introduction (draft for your revision)

*First pass from your research question and study design. Rewrite in your own words before any UC application or paper submission. Add APA citations when you revise.*

People increasingly use software to read emotion from text, including everyday journaling and chat. Those tools are convenient, but it is less clear how well an automated score matches how someone says they actually felt that day. If the match is weak, relying on the score for emotional insight could be misleading. If the match is strong, automated scoring might usefully complement self-report in personal or research settings.

This study asks one focused question: How closely does automated sentiment analysis (VADER) of daily journal entries align with self-reported affect measured by a PANAS-style Positive and Negative Affect Schedule?

The working hypothesis is that VADER's compound score for each day's journal text will show a positive association with a self-report mood index derived from the same day's PANAS-style ratings (for example, positive affect minus negative affect). A single-subject, longitudinal design lets the same person supply both measures over many days, so the comparison is within one diary stream rather than across many people at one time.

The rest of the paper describes the participant and measures, the daily procedure, the planned analyses, and (after data collection ends) how well the automated scores tracked the self-ratings, including where they diverged.
